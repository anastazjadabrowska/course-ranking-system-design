The Problem

Both major platforms treat ranking as a black box

Before designing anything, I audited how Coursera and Skillshare handle course discovery today — specifically what they expose to users and where that breaks down.

Coursera
Sort was missing entirely from search results. Users could filter, but had no way to reorder what they got. When filters were applied, nothing on screen confirmed it — filters activated silently.
Skillshare
Ratings hidden on listing cards — only visible after clicking into a course. With 30,000+ courses and no ratings on the listing page, there's no way to tell a great course from a mediocre one without clicking through each one.
Both
Filter state invisible. Users paid the price: wasted time on low-quality content, genuinely good courses staying buried.
The Algorithm

A composite score from five signals

Each course receives a Ranking Score — a weighted combination of five signals, each scored 0–100. The score drives ordering and determines which quality label the course receives.

Signal Weight Why
User RatingVolume-weighted star average 40% Prevents gaming by small review sets — a course with 3 five-star reviews shouldn't outrank one with 3,000 four-star reviews.
Completion Rate% of learners who finish 25% Hardest signal to fake — users vote with their time, not just their stars.
PopularityEnrollments + recent growth 20% Scaled enrollment — older courses don't permanently dominate the top.
Content FreshnessDate last updated 10% Critical in fast-moving fields like AI and cloud infrastructure.
Community ActivityQ&A, instructor responsiveness 5% Lowest weight — easiest to game. But signals whether the course is alive and well-supported.

Each course receives one of three quality labels — displayed next to the star rating on every card:

★ Top Rated ✦ Highly Rated · Well Rated
Key Decisions

Three choices worth explaining

1
Label over number

An earlier version showed a raw score (0–100) on every card. Scrapped it. A learner glancing at a card can act on "Top Rated" — they can't meaningfully act on 73.4 vs 71.2. The underlying score still drives ranking; the label makes that signal human-readable without adding noise.

2
Completion rate visible and sortable

Neither Coursera nor Skillshare surfaces completion rate to users. Making it visible changes the incentive structure: instructors are rewarded for building courses people actually finish, not just enroll in.

3
Instructor dashboard: hints, not weights

The roadmap includes a dashboard for course creators — but deliberately without exposing the exact weight of each factor. If instructors know rating = 40%, they optimise for rating, not for learners. And every rebalancing of weights feels like a rule change they weren't warned about. The better version: actionable hints. "63% of learners drop off before lesson 4 — review pacing in that section." Actionable, not algorithmic.
