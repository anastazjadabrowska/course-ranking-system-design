About This Project

This document is a product design case study exploring how course ranking should work on a multi-category learning platform. The brief was open-ended: define the principles of course ranking, identify the contributing factors, and design the sorting and filtering logic for a course listing page.

The deliverables include this document, an interactive lo-fi prototype, and a short Loom walkthrough. The work was completed independently over approximately two days as a self-directed design exercise. All product decisions — including the algorithm weights, UX choices, and roadmap direction — are my own.

1. The Problem Worth Solving

How courses are ranked is one of the most consequential decisions on any learning platform — get it wrong, and users waste time on outdated or low-quality content, while genuinely good courses stay buried. This document describes the principles behind a ranking system that puts the right courses at the top: what signals it uses, how they're weighted, and how users can adjust the results to fit their own priorities.

2. What the Competition Gets Wrong

Before jumping into the solution, I spent some time looking at how Coursera and Skillshare handle this today — specifically what they offer users and what frustrates them. The picture that emerged was surprisingly consistent: both platforms treat ranking as a black box, and users pay the price.

2.1 Coursera

What's available today

  • Filters: Subject, Level, Duration, Language, Learning Product type, Skills
  • Default sort: 'Best Match' — no explanation of what that means
  • User-controlled sort: mostly unavailable, especially on mobile
  • Ratings: visible on cards, but only as a raw average

Where it breaks down

A UX audit of Coursera's experience (Medium, 2023) found something that surprised me: the Sort button was simply missing from search results. Users could filter, but had no way to reorder what they got. Worse, when filters were applied, there was nothing on screen to confirm it — no chips, no indicator, nothing. Filters just activated silently when you closed the panel. The audit focused on mobile, but the lesson applies regardless of device — sort and filter state should never be invisible to the user.

What this tells us

  • Sort needs to be visible and persistent — not something users have to hunt for
  • Every active filter should show up as a chip the user can remove
  • Users should always be able to see and undo what they've done

2.2 Skillshare

What's available today

  • Filters: Level only (Beginner / Intermediate / Advanced)
  • Default sort: 'Trending' or Editor's Picks — hand-curated, no user control
  • User-controlled sort: none
  • Course ratings: hidden on listing cards — only visible after you click in

Where it breaks down

Skillshare's G2 and Trustpilot reviews tell a consistent story: people love the content once they find it, but finding it is a gamble. With 30,000+ courses and no ratings on the listing page, there's no way to tell a great course from a mediocre one without clicking through each one. The platform leans heavily on its own 'Originals' in the default ranking, which many users feel pushes independent instructors unfairly down the list.

The numbers back this up: Trustpilot sits at 3.6/5 while the App Store shows 4.8/5 — the gap suggests people enjoy the learning experience itself, but the discovery journey is where they get frustrated.

What this tells us

  • Ratings need to be on the listing card — that's the moment users are making decisions
  • If you don't give users sorting controls, they'll assume the algorithm is biased (and often they're right)
  • Transparency isn't just a nice-to-have — it's what earns trust

3. How the Ranking Score Works

The core of this design is a composite Ranking Score — a weighted combination of five signals, each scored 0–100 before being combined into a final score on the same scale. The score determines how courses are ordered and which quality label each course receives.

FactorWeightDescription & Rationale
User Rating 40% Average star rating, weighted so that courses with more reviews carry more influence than those with only a handful. Prevents a new course with one 5-star review from outranking a course with thousands of satisfied learners.
Completion Rate 25% How many enrolled learners actually finish the course — normalised for course length, since a 2-hour course will naturally complete at a higher rate than a 40-hour one. Completion is compared against the expected baseline for courses of similar duration, making it a fairer signal of genuine quality.
Popularity Signal 20% Total enrollments plus recent growth. Scaled so that dominant courses do not permanently block newer quality content from surfacing.
Content Freshness 10% How recently the course was updated. Critical for fast-moving fields like AI, cloud services, and software development frameworks.
Community Activity 5% Q&A participation, instructor responsiveness, and learner bookmarks — signals that the course is active and well-supported.

4. Sorting Options

The Ranking Score is the default — it gives most people the best results. But users don't always want 'best overall.' Sometimes they want newest, or most popular, or the course with the highest completion rate. All five sort modes update the results instantly, without a page reload.

5. Filters

Filters narrow the pool before sorting kicks in. They all work together — selecting Level: Beginner and Rating: 4.5+ means you only see beginner courses rated above 4.5, then ranked by whatever sort mode is active. Every active filter shows up as a chip at the top of results so users always know what's applied, and can remove any single filter without losing the rest.

6. Four Decisions Worth Explaining

6.1 Showing a quality label on the card

Every course card displays a quality label derived from the Ranking Score:

★ Top Rated (80–100) ✦ Highly Rated (60–79) · Well Rated (40–59)

An earlier version of the design showed the raw numeric score (0–100), but a label is more useful — a learner glancing at a card can immediately act on 'Top Rated' without having to interpret what 73.4 means relative to 71.2. The underlying score still drives the ranking; the label makes that signal human-readable without adding visual noise to the card.

For platforms operating in the EU, this also satisfies regulatory requirements. The Digital Services Act (DSA, 2022) and the Omnibus Directive (2019/2161) require platforms to explain the main parameters of their ranking systems, confirm ranking is not influenced by paid placement, and offer alternative sorting options. Our design — quality labels derived from a transparent scoring system, five user-controlled sort modes, and a footer link to a 'How Course Ranking Works' page — covers all three. For platforms outside the EU, this remains good practice even where not legally required.

6.2 Making completion rate visible

Neither Coursera nor Skillshare shows completion rate to users. Making it visible and sortable changes the incentive structure for instructors — and gives learners a signal that star ratings alone can't provide.

6.3 The active filter strip

This directly addresses the core failure from the competitor audit: Coursera applied filters silently, with no confirmation on screen. Active filter chips make state visible and reversible at all times.

6.4 A trade-off worth naming: completion rate over raw rating

An earlier version weighted User Rating at 50% and Completion Rate at 15%. I reversed the balance after thinking through incentives: rating is easy to solicit, completion is not. Prioritising completion at 25% means the algorithm rewards courses that actually deliver — at the cost of occasionally surfacing a course with a slightly lower star average that more people finish. That is a trade-off I am comfortable with.

7. What Comes Next

  • Primary success metric: time-to-first-good-course — measured as time from search to starting a course the user does not abandon within the first 20 minutes. Secondary: first-course completion rate. Both are more meaningful than click-through rate, which optimises for attractive cards, not good outcomes.
  • Personalised weights: over time, adjust which signals matter more based on each user's own completion and rating history
  • A/B testing: the five weights are assumptions right now — they should be validated against real user behaviour and adjusted per category
  • Instructor response rate as a standalone filter: currently part of Community Activity, but worth surfacing directly
  • Penalty signals: refund rates and early drop-off (within first 20 minutes) as negative inputs to the score
  • Recency boost: a temporary bump for recently updated courses, to help them build momentum faster
  • Smarter search: moving from keyword matching to meaning-based matching, so 'intro to machine learning' surfaces the right courses even if the title uses different words
  • Instructor dashboard: a dedicated view for course creators — but deliberately without exposing the exact weight of each factor. Instead of numbers, instructors would see actionable guidance: "Your course hasn't been updated in 14 months" or "63% of learners drop off before lesson 4." Showing raw percentages would create two problems: instructors would start optimising for the algorithm rather than for learners, and any rebalancing of weights would feel like a rule change they hadn't agreed to. Actionable hints avoid both.

8. A Note on the Prototype

The prototype is designed for desktop browser — a deliberate call given that course discovery is a considered, multi-step task: users compare options, apply several filters, read descriptions, and often return to the list more than once. That pattern fits a wide screen much better than mobile. A mobile version would be a natural next step, with a bottom sheet for filters and a simplified card layout, but desktop is the right canvas for demonstrating ranking and sorting logic clearly.
